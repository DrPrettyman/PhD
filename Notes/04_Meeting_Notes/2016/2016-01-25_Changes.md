# Changes

In my previous notes I summarised the method of finding the generalised
auto-correlation, as described in (williamson2015).\
In fact I had made a mistake in trying to replicate the results, I had
used a much smaller $\Delta t$, when in the paper they use
$\Delta t = 0.5$ so that in a window of length 50 there are 100 points.\
The system $$
\dot{x} &= y + \varepsilon^{(x)}\\
\dot{y} &= \mu - x^2 + \varepsilon^{(y)}\\
\mu &= 0.5-0.005t
$$ Is integrated from $t=0$ to $t=100$ and the solution
given at intervals of $\Delta t=0.5$. Note $\mu$ varies from $1/2$ at
$t=0$ to zero at $t=100$ and $\varepsilon$ is white noise with standard
deviation $\sigma = 0.01$. The results obtained by (williamson2015) are
shown in figure [3](#williamsonorbit)
, the results of our own integration are
shown in figure [4](#ourownonorbit0p01)
. Clearly, the two are not quite the
same.\
This time series of 201 points is subjected to the technique described
in (williamson2015). First the Matrix $A$ is found in a sliding window
of length 50 (i.e. 100 points) and the eigenvalues of $A$ are found in
each window. The eigenvalues $\tilde{a}_1$, $\tilde{a}_2$ (note there
are two eigenvalues because we have a 2D system) are complex conjugate
of each other, so we just look at the first eigenvalue (as given by
Matlab) and call this $\tilde{a}$.\
The eigenvalue $\tilde{a}$ is then transformed by $$
\Re(\lambda_j) &= \frac{1}{\Delta t}\log |a_j|\\
\Im(\lambda_j) &= \frac{1}{\Delta t}\phi_j
$$ to obtain $\lambda$, an eigenvalue of the Jacobian, and
$\lambda$ is then plotted in the sliding window, as in figure
[1](#Jeigenvals). The
general shape is similar to the plot in (williamson2015) (see figure
[2](#williamsoneigenvals)
) the real part oscillates around $-0.1$
and the imaginary part declines from around $0.8$ to around $0.4$.
However, the plots in figure [1](#Jeigenvals)
 are clearly much more wiggly than those in fig.
[2](#williamsoneigenvals)
, and the reason for this is a mystery.

# Differences

As noted, there is a difference between figure
[3](#williamsonorbit)
and figure [4](#ourownonorbit0p01)
. The most striking difference is that
fig. [3](#williamsonorbit)
 seems to contain much more noise. The
solution appears to be being 'kicked' from orbits close to the centre
into orbits further out, and back again. This sort of thing happens when
we increase the noise term to a standard deviation of 0.5, as shown in
figure [5](#ourownonorbit0p5)
. However, the Jacobian eigenvalues, shown
in figure [6](#Jeigenvals0p5)
, are just as wiggly as before, so even is
(williamson2015) misquoted the size of the noise, this cannot be the
source of the eigenvalue problem.
