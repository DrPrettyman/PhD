# Hopf bifurcation example

In my previous notes I summarised the method of finding the generalised
auto-correlation, as described in (williamson2015). I also included an
example analysing a time series generated by the system $$
\dot{x} &= y + \varepsilon^{(x)}\\
\dot{y} &= \mu - x^2 + \varepsilon^{(y)}\\
\mu &= 0.5-0.005t
$$ Where a homoclinic bifurcation occurs at time $t=100$.
Here I have generated some more data using the system $$
\dot{r} &= \mu r - r^3 + \varepsilon^{(r)}\\
\dot{\theta} &= 1 + r^2 + \varepsilon^{(\theta)}\\
\mu &= -2.8 + 0.05t
$$ as in (williamson2015). A Hopf bifurcation occurs at
time $t=56$ when $\mu=0$.\
In both systems $\varepsilon^{(\cdot)}$ is an independent white noise
term with standard deviation 0.01. However In the homoclinic example
this noise generated a time series which looked very different to the
noisy result pictured in (williamson2015). In fact noise with std of
around 0.5 gave a more similar looking result.\
When looking at the "Hopf" example we used several different levels of
noise, standard deviation 0.01, 0.1 and 0.5. The results are shown in
figures [1](#Hopf0p01),
[2](#Hopf0p1) and
[3](#Hopf0p5) resp.. The
example with the largest noise level (0.5, fig.
[3](#Hopf0p5)) appears most
similar to the results from (williamson2015), reproduced in figure
[4](#Hopf_williamson),
considering that the $x$ and $y$ values in the other examples are orders
of magnitude smaller. However, we will now look at the Jacobian
eigenvalues which betray this idea.\

The (real positive) eigenvalue of the Jacobian matrix (calculated from
the eigenvalues of the matrix $A$ which is estimated from the data in a
sliding window of length 60 points) is shown for the tree different
noise-level examples shown in figures
[1](#Hopf0p01)
-[3](#Hopf0p5)
. These are shown in figures
[5](#HopfJ0p01)
-[7](#HopfJ0p5)
. The corresponding results from (williamson2015)
are shown in figure [8](#HopfJeig_williamson)
. Using noise std 0.1 the results, as
far as these eigenvalues are concerned, look very similar. The imaginary
part is very close to 1.0 for the whole run and the real part increased
from -1.5 to zero, indicating critical slowing down. using smaller noise
(std 0.01, figure [5](#HopfJ0p01)
) the imaginary part is even closer to 1, but the
real part increases from -2.5 to -1.5, and it does so in a very
non-linear way. Using noise std 0.5 (which gave an $x-y$ trajectory
closest to the (williamson2015) result) the imaginary part has a much
higher variance. The real part does increase from -1.5 to 0 but again,
in a less smooth/linear way than in figure
[8](#HopfJeig_williamson)
. It seems that using a white noise term
with std in the range $(0.1, 0.5)$ might give results similar to those
in the Williamson paper.
